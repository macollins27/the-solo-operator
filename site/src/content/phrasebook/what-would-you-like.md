pasteText: |
  Make the next move.
  If you genuinely don't know what to do, say "I'm stuck on X."
  Don't kick the decision back. I hired you to make it.
afterPaste: |
  The honest version is "I don't know what to do, I'm stuck because of X." That's a real signal you can work with — you can tell the agent what X actually means, or which side of X to favor. The dishonest version is "what would you like" — a question that pushes responsibility to a person who has less context than the asker.
whyThisWorks:
  - 'An agent asking the operator what to do is almost never asking out of deference. It is asking because it has hit something it doesn''t know how to resolve and is using a polite question as a way to pause without admitting the impasse. Two non-technical hours of "what would you like" is the same as zero engineering.'
  - "Refusing the question forces the impasse to surface. Either the agent unsticks itself and moves, or it names what it's stuck on. Both outcomes produce progress. The polite-question loop produces neither."
whereThisCameFrom:
  - 'I sat in a session at midnight, exhausted, while an agent asked me "what would you like me to do?" four times in a row. I had no idea. Neither did it.'
  - 'We sat there. I picked something arbitrary. The agent did it. The work was unusable the next morning — I had been making engineering decisions while half-asleep and uninformed, because the agent had offered me the chair and I had taken it.'
  - '*Two non-technical people don''t add up to one engineer, and one of us was supposed to be the engineer.* I learned to refuse the question every time. The agent stuck at "what would you like" is the agent admitting it has nothing — and that''s information.'
