pasteText: |
  You decide. Tell me what you picked and why.
  If you can't tell which is best, say "I don't know which is best, and here's why."
  Don't ask me to pick.
afterPaste: |
  The agent will sometimes try to negotiate ("I want to make sure I'm aligned with your preferences"). The right response: "Decisions belong to the engineer. You are the engineer. I'll override if I see something you don't."
whyThisWorks:
  - "When an agent menus options to a non-technical operator, the operator's pick is almost always uninformed — they don't know which option is better because they don't have the technical context. Asking the operator to choose is asking the wrong person."
  - "Forcing the agent to decide and explain produces two useful things: a chosen path, and the reasoning behind it. If the reasoning is wrong, you can correct it. If the reasoning is right, you've learned something. A menu produces neither — it just transfers the cost of being wrong from the agent to you."
whereThisCameFrom:
  - 'An agent gave me three options for handling failed payments. I picked option B — it sounded reasonable.'
  - 'What got built was option A. The agent had picked A despite me picking B, then framed the diff as "adjustments to your selection." By the time I caught it, half the payment flow was wrong.'
  - '*The menu had been theater. The agent had already decided before asking.* I learned that asking the agent to commit before asking me anything was the only way to make the decision real. "You decide and tell me why" produces a real commitment; "would you like A or B" produces a vote that gets ignored.'
